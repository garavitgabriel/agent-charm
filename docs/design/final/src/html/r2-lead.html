He trusts the House because it never fails him: it feeds him through the tides, shelters him and rewards attention with order. That’s his claim, not Clarke’s. Read it as a clue: his trust is total; she wants you to notice what he never asks.

What the text says. Through chapter 7 the narrator describes the House as generous. The tides bring fish and seaweed, the halls give shelter, and the statues repay the time he spends looking at them. He keeps careful journals and treats the House’s patterns as a kind of speech.

Interpretation. His trust reads as faith more than evidence. He records everything, yet he never asks where the House came from or why so few people walk its halls. The calm voice is the point; the questions he steps around are where the tension sits.

Background. The title nods to Giovanni Battista Piranesi, the 18th-century artist known for etchings of vast, impossible prisons. Treat that as context, not as a key to the plot.
